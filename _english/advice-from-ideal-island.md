These suggestions are specific, practical, and connected. Some patiently explain an idea, some hit a nerve, and some make things click. They're worth coming back to. Here they are as a gift to you for 2018.

## Handling criticism

1. Nobody enjoys being criticized. Anyone who says otherwise is lying.
2. A gracious first response is to pause before explaining yourself and resist the urge to fire back.
3. Every day, or at least every week, take an honest inventory of your shortcomings and the trouble you've caused. Describe them to yourself in the strongest terms. Criticism will feel more manageable when it comes.
4. Misunderstandings cause much criticism. Build trust first, clarify the facts second, and then clear up the misunderstanding. Those three steps can resolve many of the relationship problems that arise from criticism.
5. When criticized, start by asking where you can begin to solve the problem. The question of who's right can wait.
6. Let casual remarks pass. Take substantive criticism seriously.
7. If you disagree with criticism or don't understand it, ask the person directly for their full view. Stay in the conversation.

## Single life

8. Finding a partner is unlikely to make you happy if you're unhappy on your own.
9. A good income and a future you can look forward to make it easier to enjoy single life.
10. Use the time you have on your own to build skills, continue your education, and follow your curiosity. Once you have a partner, long stretches of free time can be harder to find.
11. Find close friends you can grow alongside. Closeness makes practical support possible. Growing together helps the friendship last.
12. Build routines of your own and leave room for change. A partner will bring new routines into your life. Some people stay single longer than they want because their lives leave little space for another person.
13. Go to social events that interest you, meet strangers, and make friends.
14. Build a network of people you can call on. This could include a babysitter or cleaner, a trusted driver, a hairstylist, a trainer, a family doctor, friends or relatives you can call at any hour, and a travel agent. Let skilled people help with the parts of day-to-day life they know best.
15. Spend less time lamenting your single status, even as a joke. People often find someone more attractive when their relationship status isn't the first thing they hear about them.
16. Even if you're happy single, stay open to flirting and connection. The author sees that openness as good for body and mind.
17. Be conservative with your finances, carry adequate critical illness insurance, and look after your money.
18. Befriend people who have good marriages. Examples matter when you're making big decisions.
19. Let relationships develop in stages. Meeting someone, going on dates, becoming intimate, and dating over time can lead to a committed relationship. Each stage calls for different expectations.
20. Play sports, go out, share meals, and travel more often. Spend less time at tea gatherings, meditation retreats, or sitting alone if those activities draw you into self-pity.
21. Take the initiative, in everything.

## Personal presentation

22. For most people, looking good means looking presentable. That's within anyone's reach.
23. Watch a couple of workplace dramas like *Suits* closely. You'll get a sense of where the boundaries are for professional presentation.
24. Cleanliness is the minimum. For an office worker in a modern city, that means showering, changing clothes, and cleaning shoes every day.
25. People infer your standing from how you present yourself.
26. Ask your best-presented friend for a hairstylist recommendation, then get to know the stylist. One sign of a good hairstylist is that they offer a simple solution without steering you toward the most expensive option.
27. Keep your wardrobe small and buy the best individual pieces you can afford.
28. Find a basic style that suits you and stick with it. Even Fan Bingbing can look her best with her arms and thighs covered. Many people keep experimenting because they lack the confidence to simplify.
29. At work, showing cleavage, underarm hair, or part of your backside is about professional presentation, regardless of how attractive someone finds it.
30. Exercise helps you understand your body. Expensive clothes can't fix a slouch.
31. Clothes that follow the body's shape without squeezing it tend to look sharper on anyone.
32. The author treats losing weight as a question of discipline.
33. Looking clean from head to toe takes real care and can make anyone look well put together, including older men.
34. People who dismiss the importance of personal presentation rarely find out what a poor impression cost them.

## Big decisions

35. Do what you can to build financial security, look after your appearance, get good at things, and be someone others enjoy. More options give you more say in what happens next.
36. A decision can matter more than ability. Some call that fate. The ability to choose well and quickly at a decisive moment is a skill people often overlook.
37. Choose for what will serve you over the long term. Focusing too narrowly on the immediate payoff can lead to poor decisions. The author's example is choosing a partner with whom you'd want to raise children over choosing solely for how it feels to date them.
38. People who accomplish big things commit fully once they've made a decision.
39. Think in terms of odds. One card guarantees a million dollars. The other gives you a fifty percent chance at a hundred million dollars and a fifty percent chance at nothing. Which do you pick, and why?
40. Ask for opinions from people you genuinely admire and learn from.
41. Whatever advice you receive, the decision is yours. Take responsibility for what follows.
42. Step outside the comfort zone where everything feels safe.
43. Put impractical fantasies aside and get to work on something concrete. Spend less time thinking and more time doing.
44. When you choose a direction, consider the people you'll end up working and spending time with. This matters especially in career decisions.

## Relationships

45. Healthy relationships bring independent people together who can contribute to each other's lives. Huddling together to get by is a poor substitute.
46. A healthy relationship starts with bringing something of value to another person, even if it's small at first.
47. The author sees voluntary exchange as the kind of relationship with the most positive feedback and the least lingering obligation. Markets can be severe and generous at the same time.
48. Relationships sustained entirely by emotion can be especially fragile and damaging. Give the relationship other ways to grow. In marriage, passion fades, but you can become partners in learning or work toward a concrete goal together. Parents and children can become learning partners, too.
49. Give people room to decline your company or requests. Respect their time and what they can take on emotionally.
50. Take responsibility for yourself, your choices, and whatever comes of them.
51. Regularly reflect on the family you grew up in and your close relationships. Learn a few ways to understand yourself, then acknowledge where you struggle with others. Many of these difficulties take shape in childhood. Accepting them is a first step toward changing them, and many barriers can be overcome.
52. Let trust be your first instinct and keep thinking for yourself.
53. Relationships have depth as well as frequency. Someone you see often may still know very little about you.
54. Keep your distance from adults who expect to be looked after and people who struggle to contribute constructively.
55. Your relationships reflect something of who you really are.

## People who help you along

56. The people who change your course give the right guidance at the right moment. They show you how things really are. The path was already there, but you couldn't see it without them.
57. Generosity reflects the kind of person they have worked to become. Your own talent or what you can offer needn't be their reason for helping.
58. The best way to repay them is to work at becoming someone who helps the way they do.
59. Seek these people out. Give them a reason to enjoy having you around.
60. Approach relationships ready to learn something.
61. Good relationships can grow through small favors and timely boosts. Avoid expecting people to rescue you. Appreciate help when it comes and take stock of your own situation when it doesn't.
62. Let people see what their help made possible. Share the results and celebrate with them.
63. Sending two boxes of pastries every holiday can become routine, even over a lifetime. To thank someone, pay attention to what matters to them and choose a gift they'll remember.
64. Give the person space and let them take the initiative. One act of help can start to feel like a burden if they're expected to keep doing it for life.
65. When you can help someone, do it quietly and make it easy for them to accept. Once it's done, let them bring it up if they want to.
66. One day you may surpass people who once meant everything to you. Keep moving forward without fear or clinging to the past.

## Managing stress

67. Stress comes with being fully engaged in life. Remembering that everyone experiences it can help keep resentment in check.
68. Work stress and personal stress need to be handled where they arise. Take care where you let the pressure out.
69. Be an optimistic pessimist. Put your energy into what you can do and accept some uncertainty about the outcome.
70. Imagine the worst outcome clearly. If you couldn't live with it and the upside is tempting you to take the risk anyway, stop. A prudent person stays clear of a wall that's about to fall.
71. You can redirect your attention for a while. When the pressure becomes too much, an hour with a puzzle game or a bout of vigorous exercise can help you recover. The problem will still be there when you're ready to return to it.
72. Have a friend you can talk to openly and who can meet you as an equal. Talking may be the most effective relief because seeing the problem from another viewpoint can make it easier to understand.
73. If you can't do that, write down what is bothering you for your eyes alone. It may look less complicated on the page.
74. Face the source of pressure directly. Putting it off or going around it generally adds to the stress.
75. The author recommends taking melatonin after a week of stress-related sleep trouble and predicts depression after a month. The essay offers this as personal advice, without evidence for the timeline.
76. Avoid deals or decisions you'd have to keep secret, however tempting the payoff.
77. If you try to relieve stress through shopping binges, drinking, drugs, overeating, or reckless sex, you can wake up with a second set of problems.
78. Give the pressure a shape. Imagine it as a monster and picture landing a punch each time you deal with one piece of the problem.

## Work habits

79. Taking notes is the work habit that has helped me most. I admire people who are first to share their notes after a meeting.
80. Finishing the day's work that day can make work more satisfying.
81. Handle problems involving people promptly. A conflict rarely resolves because you hope to avoid it or expect the other person to take a hint. Address uncomfortable issues directly, in person, and early.
82. Rehearse important actions. Think through what you'll say and make contingency plans. Careful preparation reveals several ways to achieve the same goal and gives you room to adapt as you go.
83. Make a habit of debriefing as a team. Focus on what the team learned and use the review to improve how you work.
84. If you spend much of your job meeting people, cluster meetings and appointments on a few days each week. It can take less time overall and leave room for writing and internal work.
85. If you manage people, set aside time each day to talk with team members about things beyond the immediate work. Even ten minutes of casual conversation can help you build a habit of paying attention to people.
86. Study the numbers that matter every day. Use them to ask good questions.
87. Put your phone away when talking to someone face to face.
88. In meetings, learn to bring unproductive conversations to a close and get to the point. Work toward agreement on what to do.
89. Record appointments accurately and arrive on time.
90. A rough plan often fits reality better and is easier to put into practice than a detailed one.
91. Prepare for each event you attend or speak at. Tailor what you say to the occasion. Give the organizer your public biography and materials on time, and send your talk early.
92. Set three times a day, morning, midday, and evening, to deal with social media, email, and other messages. Batching them saves time.
93. If you're a manager, learn to ask “What do you think?” and listen to the answer.
94. Buy the best equipment you can for your work.
95. Keep looking for a good partner. Work with smart people.

## Personal growth

96. Sooner or later, you'll notice how much of what you see in other people reflects the way you think.
97. At the same time, you'll see how much of your world comes from other people. The “true self” you've been searching for is a very small part of it.
98. Growth begins when you can hold both of those ideas at once.
99. The hard part is raising your standards over time. One way to keep doing that is to meet people who set better examples.
100. Be selective about whose abilities you admire. Look ahead and spend time with people whose skills you can learn from.
101. Let go of what has passed. Keep moving forward without nostalgia or regret.
102. Learn from real people you know. Having them in your life gives you a standard you have to live up to every day.
103. Look for work that feels like a calling and be willing to take it on. Resist settling too early for a life that's merely comfortable.
104. Be conscientious even when nobody is watching. Keep your mind clear.
105. In a quiet moment each day, ask what you did better than yesterday. It might be as simple as thanking a delivery worker more sincerely.
106. Look for something constructive to say. Sarcasm, complaints, blame, arguments, boasts, and idle judgments rarely help.
107. Take careful notes about your interactions with others and think them over.
108. Read broadly. If you studied the humanities, try some science. If you studied science, try literature or art. Reading helps you understand people.
109. Teaching an apprentice is a powerful way to make yourself grow.
110. Question the idea that there's a right age to start. Apart from having children, the author sees age as little reason to give up on things you want to do.

## Opportunities and traps

111. The biggest traps can look like opportunities.
112. Ask how many people stand to benefit. The more people who gain, the more likely it is to be a real opportunity. If the benefits all flow to you, think twice.
113. Ask how easy the work is. Some things take off immediately and lose their appeal as you go. Others are hard to start and become more demanding as you get better. The first kind can be a trap. The second can be an opportunity. Make steady progress on substantial work and be wary of easy shortcuts.
114. Consider the stake of whoever is urging you on. If the project is spare-time work and a financial bonus for them but requires all your energy and everything you own, treat it with caution.
115. Approval matters less than actual help. If more people lend a hand once you start, that points to an opportunity. If support disappears, it may be a trap.
116. Projects that exist mainly because resources are available can turn into traps. Work that follows a genuine shift is more likely to reveal an opportunity.
117. Work that creates a new way for more people to earn a living can be an opportunity. Exploiting gaps that benefit only you can be a trap.
118. If a larger player can easily take the rewards once you've done the work, be careful. An opportunity can grow into something that stands on its own. A pumpkin doesn't grow on a tree. Even if you could get one to grow there, its weight would be hard to support.
119. Beware of projects that leave lasting problems even if they succeed. An opportunity can leave you with more skill and a stronger reputation even if the project itself fails.
120. Projects you can pursue openly can be opportunities. Projects you have to hide from everyone can be traps.
121. Think about whether your children would be proud of what you did when they grow up.
